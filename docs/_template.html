Sampling-based Certified Planning with Graphs of Convex Sets

Planners built on graphs of convex sets trust that their regions are collision-free. The generator only promises that probabilistically, and nobody checks the answer. We measured what that costs, and built a planner that certifies every trajectory it returns.

Peng Xie and Amr Alanwar · Technical University of Munich · p.xie@tum.de

ReadThe paper in plain words

A robot arm plans motions in its configuration space: one axis per joint, seven for one arm, fourteen for two. A family of planners first carves that space into convex regions that are known to be free of collisions, then plans inside the regions, where the mathematics is clean and the trajectory is collision-free by construction. The catch sits in the word known.

The fine print on the regions

The regions are grown by a generator that cannot prove a region is free. Obstacles in configuration space are non-convex, so no finite number of cuts can exclude them exactly. The generator tests instead: it draws random samples inside the candidate region and accepts it once a batch comes back clean. That test is a promise about volume: with high confidence, at most a small fraction of the region is in collision. The fraction is small but not zero, and the sliver it tolerates, which we call the residual, hugs the region boundary, exactly where trajectories cross from one region into the next.

Why a small error becomes a large one

A planner does not sample the region at random. It looks for the shortest trajectory the model allows, and wherever the model is slightly too generous, the shortest trajectory moves there. On a 14-DOF dual-arm library built at the generator's default settings we measured 3.2% of interface samples in collision. Fed to a state-of-the-art search-based GCS planner, that volume error turned into a 62% answer error: 18 of 29 pick-and-place queries returned trajectories that drive the arms through the shelves, 39 mm deep at the median and 91 mm at the worst, every one reported as a success. The effect grows with dimension. On a controlled family of planar arms with everything fixed except the number of joints, the volume statistic reads exactly zero up to five joints while delivered answers already collide from three joints on.

Repairing the library does not work

The obvious response is to make the generator stricter. We ran three versions of that idea to completion. A ten times stricter acceptance contract kept 47 of 222 seeds and left them pairwise disjoint: a library that supports no planning at all. Sums-of-squares certified regions, the one route to a real proof, covered a third of the free space on a 2-DOF testbed and answered none of thirty queries. Uniform safety margins removed connections faster than they removed the residual. Certainty about a volume has to be paid wherever the volume meets obstacles, and in a cluttered scene that is also where regions must meet each other.

Certify the answer instead

Our planner accepts the library as it is and moves the guarantee to the trajectory. It samples the interfaces between regions, the places a trajectory must cross, and checks every sample against the true collision geometry before it enters the roadmap. An admissible informed bound discards every interface that cannot lie on a path shorter than the current best. Each candidate path is then verified continuously: one clearance query certifies a whole ball of configurations, and a chain of overlapping balls certifies the segment with no resolution parameter for a sliver to hide in. A failed stretch is repaired by a short detour inside its own convex region, and the convex polish that shortens the final path goes back through the same verification, because a polish is an optimizer too.

Where the reference answer is legal, certification costs nothing and changes nothing: our path matches it to four decimal places. Where the reference passes through an obstacle, the certified detour costs a median 9% more length, which is the price of not hitting the shelf. And where no certified path exists in the library, the planner says so instead of guessing.
